<section class="astro-description">
  <h2>IC 1396 and Mu Cephei — Herschel's Garnet Star</h2>

  <p>Located in the constellation <strong>Cepheus</strong>,
  <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IC_1396">IC 1396</a>
  is a vast emission nebula and active stellar nursery approximately
  <strong>2,400 light-years from Earth</strong>. Its glowing hydrogen
  clouds are illuminated by massive young stars, while dense regions
  of gas and dust, including the famous Elephant's Trunk Nebula,
  continue to give birth to new stars.
  <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/multi-wavelength-views-of-protostars-in-ic-1396/">NASA observations</a>
  and <a href="https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-3881/ac2cc0/pdf">astronomical research</a>
  have revealed young protostars embedded within these clouds.</p>

  <p>Appearing near IC 1396 in our sky is the magnificent
  <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu_Cephei">Mu Cephei (&mu; Cephei)</a>,
  famously known as <strong>Herschel's Garnet Star</strong> for its
  striking reddish-orange color. Located approximately
  <strong>1,307 light-years from Earth</strong>, this enormous red
  supergiant is one of the largest stars visible to the naked eye,
  with an estimated radius over 1,000 times that of our Sun, although
  estimates vary. Mu Cephei is also a semiregular variable star
  whose brightness changes as its vast outer atmosphere pulsates.</p>

  <p>Although they appear close together in the night sky,
  <strong>Mu Cephei lies considerably closer to Earth than IC 1396</strong>
  and is not physically part of the nebula. Together, they offer a
  fascinating contrast between the birth of new stars within IC 1396
  and the late evolutionary stages of Mu Cephei, a massive stellar
  giant that may eventually end its life as a supernova.</p>
